# Sensitivity & compliance

Source: TAG's RL Copy Master Guide (D&I Guidelines + Social Guidelines tabs). This is risk-mitigation content — read it before writing copy that touches casting, cultural references, body imagery, or campaigns using American iconography. The source document is much longer (case studies, legal citations); this is the condensed, actionable version. If a real situation is genuinely ambiguous, say so to the user rather than guessing.

## Banned or cautioned words/phrases in copy
- **Camouflage** — avoid.
- **"Color"** as a descriptor — don't use when models (particularly BIPOC models) are in the photo; fine when describing a product alone.
- **"Easy"** — avoid next to a photo of a woman; use "Ease" or "Effortless" instead.
- **"Elegant"** — flagged as overused specifically for Collection/Purple Label per internal creative-director feedback; reach for a more specific word.
- **"Leather"** — avoid (flagged without an explicit reason in the source; treat as a caution, ask if unsure why it matters for the specific campaign).
- **"Our Icon"** — banned buzz phrase.
- **"Timeless sophistication"** — banned buzz phrase.
- Off-brand/generic "luxury" adjectives: **chic, classy, audacious, unconventional, elegant, timeless** — and **luxury/luxurious** outright, and **iconic** except when literally describing the Polo Shirt or the Polo Bear.
- Don't overwrite — avoid excessive adjectives, florid description, and adverbs.

## Casting / model-description language
**Do say**: different, captivating, warm · natural, confident · "a good addition for our story/brand/messaging" · diversity of bodies, inclusion of all sizes · people with disabilities, disability community.
**Don't say**: "exotic look" · "we need more colored models" · "Black faces, Yellow faces, Latinx faces" · "plus size / curvy / lean model" (as a casting-request category) · "is that your real hair" (don't question natural hair) · "that's an urban look" / "ethnic look" · "smaller persons" · "you look so young" (no generational bias) · any slur, ever.

Five general principles for inclusive language: keep an open mind; focus on the person, not the demographic group they belong to (only mention age/background/disability/etc. when actually relevant); consider context (workplace-acceptable ≠ automatically brand-acceptable); when in doubt, ask rather than guess; if corrected, don't get defensive — "I'm sorry, it wasn't my intention to offend you" beats explaining your intent.

## Cultural appropriation — general rule
Never take intellectual property, traditional dress, symbols, or artifacts from someone else's culture without permission — especially from a minority or historically-oppressed group, and especially anything sacred. **Do**: take inspiration from a culture's aesthetics (color, geometry, pattern) rather than copying it directly; engage with the culture to understand its symbolism; cast a diverse range of models rather than dressing one ethnicity as another; partner directly with designers from that culture when the collection draws on it. **Don't**: darken a model's skin or dress someone in another culture's dress to "become" that culture; turn a sacred or ceremonial object into decoration or an accessory; use a culturally-specific detail without the community it belongs to being represented in its execution.

Specific cautions on file:
- **Native American** — eagle feathers (any species, all sacred) and feathered headdresses/war bonnets are off-limits entirely. No figurative/photographic likeness of a Native person without permission. Never use tribe names as product names (many are trademarked — Urban Outfitters lost a multi-year suit to the Navajo Nation over this). Sacred animals (e.g. the buffalo/Tatanka) and deities vary by tribe and must be researched, not assumed. Vintage imagery that romanticizes stereotypes is off-limits. Acceptable: inspiration from geometric/floral motifs and color combinations, celebrating the modernity (not just the past) of Native culture, and partnering directly with Native designers.
- **African** — styling non-Black models with cornrows or dreadlocks has drawn major backlash for other brands (Marc Jacobs SS17, Valentino SS16) — handle with real caution and consider whether the styling choice needs a different execution entirely.
- **South Asian** — using a bindi as costume/makeup on a model who isn't part of that religious tradition is disrespectful, not decorative.
- **Mexican/Indigenous Mexican** — copying a traditional pattern (e.g. Oaxacan embroidery) directly is plagiarism, not homage — this has triggered real protests against other designers.

## Body image
RL wants to promote positive body image — never digitally distort a model's proportions (shrinking a waist, exaggerating a thigh gap, etc.). Real precedents to know: Ann Taylor Loft, Victoria's Secret, and Vogue Brazil all faced public backlash for exactly this. Some markets have binding legal requirements, not just brand preference: **France** requires a "photographie retouchée" disclaimer on retouched ads and a health certificate for models; **Israel** bans Photoshop-based body manipulation in fashion media outright; **Norway** (Trondheim) restricts city-property ads that could contribute to negative body image.

## American flag usage (only relevant when a campaign uses actual flag imagery)
Never decorate, fold for display, use as a ceiling covering, use as a receptacle, mark with any insignia/text/image, or let it touch the ground. Never use an actual flag as apparel, bedding, or drapery — if clothing is *styled* to resemble the flag, keep it tasteful (no distressed/damaged-flag imagery, no added marks) and, if a flag motif is depicted, it must show 50 stars and 13 stripes, no other count. Never use the flag for advertising purposes directly (printed on cushions, napkins, disposable items). Military/DoD seals and unit insignia are protected by law and require prior permission — never use them in a way that could imply official endorsement.

## Regional content restrictions (Middle East)
Content for Gulf/Pan-Arab markets faces real, enforced restrictions that vary by country — Saudi Arabia/Pan-Arab titles are strictest (shoulders, chest, belly button, legs above the knee, and underarms must be covered; no physical contact shown between men and women), Kuwait is moderate, UAE/Qatar/Oman/Bahrain vary by review, Lebanon is comparatively permissive. Every visual for these markets should be checked before launch rather than assumed compliant — restrictions are also loosening over time, so don't rely on old guidance without reconfirming.

## Social copy character limits (as of the source's last update — reconfirm current platform limits before relying on these)
- **Instagram**: organic feed caption best under ~750 characters (10+ hashtags helps impressions more than 1); hard caption limit 2,200 characters, 30 hashtags, 150-character bio.
- **TikTok**: 150-character post cap including hashtags; 80-character profile description.
- **Facebook**: best-performing organic post length ~40–80 characters; hard cap is enormous (63,206) so length is a style choice, not a constraint.
- **Twitter/X**: best practice 71–100 characters per post; hard cap 280.
- **YouTube**: titles ~70 characters (100 tolerated before truncation in search); descriptions up to 5,000.
- **Snapchat**: organic ~31 characters.

**Brands we admire** (reference points named in the source guide) — for engagement rates: @Dior, @LouisVuitton, @Gucci; for brand tone specifically: @Chanel and @MiuMiu.

**Polo social approval steps** (role-based, from brief to post): social briefs the wireframes with copy CC'd → social/design/copy attend a joint design work session → creative builds the social plan → creative and copy align on story copy → creative review at the Polo social alignment meeting → creative implements feedback → social uploads final approved creative to Opal → copywriter submits copy through Opal for social-lead review → editorial reviews with the copy team lead → vetting review → a copyeditor does a final proofread → social approves and posts.

**Luxe social approval steps** (same shape, fewer/different stakeholders — roles only, not names, since who holds each role changes): design creates the grids → copy drafts the captions → an editorial reviewer adds proofing/oversight notes → the copy lead reviews and approves → design flows the approved copy into the grid deck → the Luxe creative director signs off → the relevant brand-side team gives final notes → upload to Opal → one last proofread pass before launch.
